UK Annual Statement of Trade · exports to British North America, 1870–1900 · preliminary

Canada Through the Entrepôt

What Canada consumed of the world by way of Britain: the goods Britain made and shipped, and the colonial and foreign goods it landed, warehoused and sent on — with the latter traced back, through the same volumes' import tables, to the regions they came from.

Own-year witnesses only, all repairs applied (state of 18 Aug 2026). 1871 has no volume; 1897–1900 come from wide comparative tables and are the least reliable years. Values are declared values at British ports, current £.

Two lenses on one flow

The Statement keeps two tables for what left Britain for Canada. British and Irish produce is what Britain made — woollens, iron and steel, cottons, haberdashery — and it is the bulk of the trade, £223 million over the thirty years. Foreign and colonial merchandise re-exported is what Britain merely handled: tea, silks, wine, hemp, spices, wool, rice, dried fruit, £25 million. The second table records only where the goods went, never where they had come from. But the same volumes' import tables record where Britain got each commodity in each year, so the via-Britain flow can be redistributed over those origins. That redistribution is an estimate — it assumes the tea reshipped to Montreal drew on the same sources as Britain's tea imports at large — and it inherits the Statement's own frame: origins are the ports goods were consigned from, so Holland and Hong Kong stand in for the hinterlands behind them.

Made in Britain

The British-produce side is the familiar industrial basket. Textiles and metals dominate throughout: woollens and worsteds, iron and steel (rails above all in the railway-building years), cottons, haberdashery and millinery, ready-made clothing, linens. Below the top four the list is long — refined sugar, zinc, telegraph wire, hardware, silks, earthenware, hats, oils, machinery — and it is this long tail, more than the headline textiles, that Canadian household consumption of British manufactures lives in.

Through Britain: what, and from where

Tea is the re-export trade to Canada. At £9.3 million it is 37% of everything Britain forwarded, larger than every British-produce commodity except woollens, iron and cottons. Behind it come dried fruit, silk manufactures, skins and furs, hemp, raw wool, foreign woollens, rice, spices and wine. Redistributed over Britain's import origins, the via-Britain basket is Asian first — India, Ceylon and Southeast Asia 25%, China and Japan 21% — and European second at 31%, with North America (skins, furs, hams and tallow consigned from the United States, some of it Canadian fur coming home) at 6% and Australasia (wool) at 4%.

Tea: the Statement's clearest story

The origin panel moves for one reason above all others: the British tea trade shifted from China to India and Ceylon, and whatever tea Canada bought through London and Liverpool moved with it. In the UK's tea imports of 1872 China supplied 84%; by 1892 Bengal supplied 48% and Ceylon 30%; by 1897 China was under 10%. The Canadian side tells a second story on top of that. Tea re-exported from Britain to Canada declines — about £560,000 in 1872, £300,000–490,000 through the 1880s, £130,000–200,000 by the late 1890s — even as the re-export basket as a whole grows. Canada was not drinking less tea; it was buying it less through Britain. The years match the growth of direct shipment (Indian and Ceylon tea consigned to Montreal, and from 1886 the Pacific route to Vancouver), which is exactly the kind of movement an entrepôt-only view of Canadian consumption cannot see. The redistribution below shows what the entrepôt held; it does not know what any particular chest held, and it cannot see the chests that never came through Britain.

What this does and does not show

Read as a first pass. The re-export origins are an allocation, not an observation: the Statement records destination for re-exports and origin for imports and never both on one line. Where a commodity's origin mix is stable (tea, rice, spices) the allocation is safe; where the mix is broad (silk manufactures across France, Switzerland via Holland, China, Japan) it is only indicative.

Consignment is not production. Holland, Belgium, Hong Kong, Gibraltar are ports of transit in the import tables. "Europe" here is partly the Continent's re-consignment of goods from further away.

The British-produce side hides a second layer of reach. Britain's cottons were spun from American, Indian and Egyptian cotton; its woollens from Australian and River Plate wool; its refined sugar was West Indian and Brazilian cane. Following those inputs through the import tables is the natural next step and would roughly double the traced foreign content of Canada's British imports.

Data state. Own-year sections close on their printed totals in 63.8% of cases (80% within 5%); the Canada series has been repaired for the row-slip, label-merge, capture and fused-section classes; a handful of known one-year defects remain (listed in the project notes). 1897–1900 are the weakest years.

Method

Sources: reports/canada_explorer.json (Canada value by commodity and year, both flows, own-year witnesses, all repairs) and exports/map_slim.json (UK import origins per commodity per year, curated). Script: scripts/analyze_canada_reach.py; outputs reports/canada_reach.json, canada_reach_origins.csv, canada_reach_commodities.csv. Regions by gazetteer coordinates with hand overrides for the Mediterranean, Bermuda, the Mascarenes and the Pacific.
